Every budget is a contract between a state and its people.

It decides who pays for the state, and who the state serves.

Lebanon’s 2027 draft budget, now before Parliament, balances on paper at around $7 billion.

A balance answers one question. This story follows the two it leaves open.

Chapter 01

A balanced budget. What does it tell us?

On paper, the books close. That is where the questions begin.

Start with the size of the state. In 2019, public expenditure stood at about $15.2 billion.

Then came the financial collapse. By 2026, spending had fallen to $6 billion.

The 2027 draft budget would spend around $7 billion.

Chart as text: Public expenditure

Horizontal bar chart of public expenditure: about $15.2 billion in 2019, $6 billion in 2026, and around $7 billion in the 2027 draft budget.

And on paper, it balances. Projected revenue: around $7 billion. Projected spending: around $7 billion. A projected deficit of zero.

But a zero deficit is not a clean slate. The state’s debt and accumulated liabilities remain unaddressed.

Nor does the balance say anything about who finances the state, or who benefits from it.

Chapter 02

Who pays?

The tax burden has shifted toward the households with the least.

Compare two groups of Lebanese households.

The poorest half earn income equal to 11% of GDP. In 2019, they paid 20% of all taxes. By 2026, they paid 26%.

At the other end, the richest 1% earn income equal to 20% of GDP and hold about one-third of national wealth.

Over the same years, their share of all taxes fell from 14% to 7%.

Chart as text: Share of all taxes paid

Slope chart of the share of all taxes paid. The poorest half of households rose from 20% in 2019 to 26% in 2026. The richest 1% fell from 14% to 7%.

Look at what Lebanon taxes. About 79% of tax revenue comes from consumption taxes, collected on what households buy.

Income, profits and wealth remain largely untaxed.

Chart as text: Where tax revenue comes from

Part-to-whole bar: consumption taxes account for about 79% of tax revenue; all other taxes make up the remainder.

The 2027 draft continues this pattern. It projects total revenue rising by 14.2%.

Where would the new money come from? Fuel taxes and VAT account for more than half of the additional tax revenue.

Exemptions stay in place without justification, and revenue targets rest on optimistic assumptions amid a contracting economy.

The 2027 draft budget projects total revenue rising by 14.2%. Fuel taxes and VAT account for more than half of the additional tax revenue.

Chapter 03

Who benefits from public spending?

Less spending reaches people, and less of it reaches the poorest.

Now follow the money out.

Public spending that can be traced to the people it benefits fell from $13 billion in 2019 to $4.3 billion in 2026.

Chart as text: Public spending traceable to beneficiaries

Horizontal bar chart: public spending traceable to beneficiaries fell from $13 billion in 2019 to $4.3 billion in 2026.

For the poorest half of households, the amount fell from $814 million to $617 million.

What still reached them came mostly through public hospitalization and social protection.

Chart as text: Spending reaching the poorest half of households

Horizontal bar chart: spending reaching the poorest half of households fell from $814 million in 2019 to $617 million in 2026, mostly through public hospitalization and social protection.

And for every $100 of traceable spending in 2026, youth and families received $15.

Chapter 04

Where does the proposed spending increase go?

The 2027 draft spends more. Almost all of the increase goes to one line.

The 2027 draft raises spending. More than 90% of that increase goes to personnel costs.

That would make personnel costs 65% of the entire budget.

Capital spending, meanwhile, would rise by only around $25 million, in a country with vast reconstruction and infrastructure needs.

Resources that reach people directly remain limited.

Chapter 05

What should Lebanon’s budget do over the next five years?

A budget can balance and still leave the hardest questions unanswered.

Over the next five years, Lebanon’s budget will have to answer four.

  1. 1How should Lebanon raise and allocate public resources?
  2. 2Who should bear the cost?
  3. 3Who should benefit?
  4. 4What reforms would make those choices possible?

From shortcomings to a roadmap

TPI’s roundtable brings policymakers, public officials and experts together to move the debate from what the budget gets wrong to what it should become.

  1. 01

    A shared starting point

    Participants will use TPI’s research on who pays taxes and who benefits from public spending as a common evidence base.

  2. 02

    Concrete options

    They will debate options for taxation, exemptions, spending and public financial management.

  3. 03

    Feasibility and trade-offs

    They will examine the feasibility and trade-offs of reforms, including their effects on vulnerable households.

The question ahead

What should the budget be?

Areas of agreement and open questions from the roundtable will inform TPI’s forthcoming report of the same name.
